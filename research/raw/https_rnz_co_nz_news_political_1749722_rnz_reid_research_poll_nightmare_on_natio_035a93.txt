RNZ-Reid Research poll: Nightmare on National Street while Greens soar
Jo Moir
Political editor
Caption:
National's Christopher Luxon (L) and Labour's Chris Hipkins.
Photo credit:
RNZ
Political editor Jo Moir analyses latest RNZ poll
Morning Report
6 October 2026
Queue

Analysis - Nothing in the latest RNZ-Reid Research poll will give either of the Chrises any confidence as they head into their first head-to-head debate on Tuesday night.

Not since the nightmare 2020 election when National bled 23 of its seats has the party's polling been as bad as Tuesday's result.

With just over four weeks until the election the governing party has polled at just 25.9 percentage points, only marginally higher than the 25.6 it collected six years ago when Judith Collins was leader and the party was in an Opposition spiral.

Christopher Luxon took over as party leader in November of the following year - only a year after entering Parliament and eight months after making his maiden speech - and just five years later he's fighting to keep the prime ministership and not become the first government since the 1970s to survive just one term in office.

If the coalition is voted out come 7 November it would be the first one-term National government ever - history making of the worst kind.

As the saying goes, hindsight is a wonderful thing, but there are MPs within National and supporters amongst its base asking whether the call to keep Luxon on as leader in August when his position was challenged by former minister Chris Penk, was the right one.

The party no longer has time to debate that or do anything about it.

At this point Luxon is the leader they decided was their best bet for an election win and they all have to see it through.

The polling is bouncing around for National - since July reputable public polling has had the party anywhere between 27 and 31.

The last RNZ-Reid Research poll in August, which captured the period immediately after Luxon survived a second coup attempt, had National on 29.1 percentage points.

National Party internal polling, which tracks movements much more regularly and can define bumps and dips connected to specific events, claims to have the party closer to 30 at this point in the cycle.

RNZ's poll was out in the field from 24 September until Thursday night and didn't capture the mud slinging between National and Labour over the release of the latter's fiscal plan and whether or not it added up.

And while National is wallowing in the mid-20s it's not all sunshine and rainbows for Labour either.

The party was also down 1.4 points on our August poll to 30.8.

That gives a total purple vote - the combined results of National and Labour - of just 56.7 percent support, down from 61.3 percent in August and 64.9 percent at the 2023 election.

What this poll suggests is that the relatively negative campaign being run by both parties about who is lying, who is taxing, and who is being irresponsible with taxpayers' money is not resonating with as many voters as they might like.

The party campaign launches at the end of last month were largely dominated by each leader talking at length about the other and laying out everything that was wrong with them.

This election will be fought on the cost of living and whether voters think they'll be better off with one side or the other.

A tit-for-tat of name-calling and at times misinformation could encourage voters to simply look elsewhere or tune out altogether.

With just 61 percent of young people enrolled to vote there's clearly work to be done tuning some people in.

Love for the major parties is dying a death and the so-called minor parties are reaping the rewards.

The Greens are flying high on 14.8, up almost five points, while ACT is also up on nine, and Opportunity has edged up ever so slightly to 5.5.

It's only New Zealand First and Te Pāti Māori who have taken a slight hit, down to 10.6 and two respectively.

In the case of the Greens they seem to be maximising on a vacuum that was created by Labour as the party charged through the year holding back on policy and any real sell for what an alternative government would look like.

The Greens have in recent months been putting out costed policy and capturing headlines by talking about the cost of living and environmental issues - it's bread and butter stuff for the Greens, but has increasingly been getting cut-through online where the party has a strong social media game.

It's also now distanced enough from the chaos and turmoil the party endured in the first half of the term with a string of candidates dropping out of Parliament due to various scandals.

Opportunity is on the rise too as a party not too dissimilar to the Greens but putting its hand up as a party prepared to work with either side - the only other party in Parliament that has managed to pull that off over the years is New Zealand First.

With Winston Peters having ruled out working with Labour that kingmaker position has disappeared.

While Luxon has ruled out working with Opportunity, there's plenty of scepticism that he would choose to go into Opposition rather than pick up the phone to leader Qiulae Wong.

There isn't bad blood and history between those two parties in the same way there is between Labour and New Zealand First.

Also no National supporter would thank Luxon for gifting Labour the halls of power because of a rule-out comment he made months ago.

On Tuesday evening both Luxon and Hipkins will have the first opportunity in TVNZ's leader debate to go head-to-head on the issues that matter most to New Zealanders.

Luxon heads into that debate with a net favourability score of negative 23.7, while Hipkins has joined him in the negatives for the second time in this poll series, registering negative 3.6.

Both have their work cut out for them in the weeks ahead.

Reid Research surveyed 1000 eligible voters online between 24 September and 1 October 2026, using quota sampling and weighting by age, gender and geography. The poll had a reported maximum margin of error of +/- 3.1 percentage points at a 95 percent confidence level. Party-vote figures excluded the 3 percent who were undecided or did not know and the 2.6 percent who said they would not vote.
