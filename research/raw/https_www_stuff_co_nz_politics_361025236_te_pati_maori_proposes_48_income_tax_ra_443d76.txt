Te Pāti Māori has launched a Kiwi Tax Plan, proposing a 48% top tax rate and a rebate for New Zealanders earning less than $60,000.

The policy also proposes a wealth tax, increased company tax rate, land banking tax, vacant house tax and stamp duty.

The party claims its plan would mean 4.5 million New Zealanders pay less income tax, and around 4.2 million people would receive an additional $4000 a year.

“This is a policy for everyone. Whether you are a cleaner, a nurse, a teacher, a tradie, a pensioner or raising a whānau, we want you to keep more of what you earn,” a party spokesperson said.

The proposed changes are:

Transform the income tax system: Te Pāti Māori would remove tax completely on income earned below $30,000. Those earning up to $60,000 would see a decreased tax rate of 15% (down from the 17.5% that currently applies to income earned between $15,601 and $53,500).

Income earned between $60,001 and $90,000 would be taxed at 33%, $90,001 to $180,000 at 39%, $180,001 to $300,000 at 42% and income over $300,001 would have a new tax rate of 48%.

Currently, the highest income tax rate is 39% for Kiwis earning over $180,001 a year.

Tax rebate for kai: Dubbed “no GST on kai”, rather than actually removing GST from food, this policy would offer an income tax credit to people earning $60,000 or less.

The party says this would benefit 64% of Aotearoa, and return enough money to Kiwis’ pockets to fund eight weeks of kai each year.

Asked how much the policy would return to people’s pockets, party co-leader Rawiri Waititi said it would “be worth around about $1000 per person in that particular tax bracket”.

Wealth tax: The party is proposing a tax for anyone who owns more than $2 million in net assets. The party said this targets 3% of New Zealand’s wealthiest individuals.

This is stronger than the wealth tax proposed by the Green Party, which would impose a 2.5% tax on net assets exceeding $10m, excluding the family home.

Te Pāti Māori’s policy has no family home exemption, and would introduce a marginal tax rate.

This would mean a 1.5% wealth tax for net assets exceeding $2m, 2% tax for assets exceeding $5m and a 2.5% tax for net assets over $10m.

Party co-leader Debbie Ngarewa-Packer said they expected this would bring in $8.8 billion in government revenue.

Return the company tax rate to 33%: The company tax rate is currently 28%.

This also goes further than the Greens’ policy, which would increase the tax rate to 33% for 0.7% of New Zealand’s biggest businesses.

Under Te Pāti Māori, the 33% tax rate would apply across the board.

The party would also invest $1b into the Inland Revenue Department, the Serious Fraud Office, the Financial Markets Authority and the NZ Financial Intelligence Unit to eliminate tax avoidance practices.

International Profit Transfer Tax: Te Pāti Māori would introduce a 5% tax on the value of profits transferred offshore.

No further detail was provided on the bounds of this policy, but New Zealand does already tax interest, dividends, and royalties paid to foreign investors.

Land banking tax: A 33% tax on land being held for future development would discourage land banking, support the productive use of land and bring in $2b, the party says.

The party’s co-leaders confirmed this would kick in if someone owned land for 12 months or longer without taking any steps towards obtaining consent or development.

Vacant house tax: The party would also implement a 2% tax on vacant houses, which it says would free up the 111,700 empty homes across Aotearoa and bring in $1.7b.

Waititi confirmed a house would be considered vacant if it hadn’t been used over a 12-month period.

Stamp duty: Te Pāti Māori would introduce a 5% tax on residential property sales, with the only exclusion being first home purchases worth less than $1m.

Ngarewa-Packer said this would bring in $2.1b, with the whole tax package providing the government a $4b boost in tax revenue (based on a $20b increased tax take, but $16b in additional expenditure).

“That means that we're not having to borrow to do this, and I think that was the really big picture, that was the challenge,” she said.

“Why we've taken so long to study and wānanga on this with the experts is that we want to be in a situation where we can make this happen and not hear the argument that we're going to have to borrow billions.”

Waititi said having a conversation about a transformational tax policy will be a bottom line for the party in any coalition negotiations.

National’s campaign chair, Simeon Brown. Photo: Bruce Mackay / The Post

National’s campaign chair was quick to react to the announcement, holding an impromptu press conference within one hour of the policy’s release.

“What today's announcement confirms is that every single part of Chris Hipkins’ coalition wants to increase your taxes. The only way to stop this and to ensure no new taxes is to party vote National,” Simeon Brown said.

“I mean, Chris Hipkins had a bed tax he was adding to his nine yesterday as well. But ultimately, as I said, every part of Chris Hipkins’ coalition wants to increase taxes on working New Zealanders.”

He quoted policies from the Greens, Labour and Te Pāti Māori, as well as Labour’s proposed capital gains tax and and small business action plan.

“Chris Hipkins is going around saying the only tax he's taking to this year's election is a capital gains tax. Well, that is frankly untrue,” he said.

“We know that Te Pāti Māori is now ganging up with the Green Party, in terms of their coalition negotiations based on the press conference this afternoon, to ensure that there will be more taxes if Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity are elected.”
