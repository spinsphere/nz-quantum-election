The odds rise when a kingmaker gets expensive or no bloc can reach a majority — but a pact to exclude the minors needs National and Labour to hold 62 seats between them, so a big enough minor-party surge makes it impossible rather than likely. The chart tracks that arithmetic on the polling average since 2023; in 98% of today's simulations the two still clear the line. Luxon's August-2026 call for an MMP referendum doesn't move this either way — rewriting the rules to shrink the minor parties is an alternative to governing with Labour, not a step toward it, and Labour opposed the idea.

5 OCT 2026

Labour and National opened the campaign's second week duelling over motorists, with Labour pledging a fuel-price crackdown and 'right to repair' for cars while National committed $800 million to extend the Christchurch Northern Motorway.

Both major parties targeted drivers on Monday: Labour unveiled a 'Fair Go for Kiwi Drivers' package of fuel-market competition measures and a right to repair, while National promised to fully fund the Belfast to Pegasus Motorway and Woodend Bypass in its first Budget. The weekend's fiscal-plan fight escalated, with Labour finance spokesperson Barbara Edmonds directly accusing Nicola Willis of lying over the alleged $8.5b pay-equity hole. The Greens' Auckland level-crossing announcement was dramatically interrupted by a near miss between a car and a train, and a catch-up MATA-Horizon poll showed the Greens edging Labour for the Māori-roll party vote — a potential problem for Te Pāti Māori's candidate-vote-only strategy.

ISSUES
Labour targets fuel prices and pledges 'right to repair' for cars

Labour announced a cost-of-living package for motorists: car manufacturers would be required to give independent mechanics fair access to the manuals, software, tools and information needed to fix modern cars; the Commerce Commission would report weekly on fuel prices and whether falling costs are passed on; Labour's proposed price-gouging law would apply to wholesale and retail fuel; and independent petrol stations would get fairer access to fuel supplies. Hipkins framed the policy around the cost of living, with commerce spokesperson Arena Williams saying independent workshops currently turn away work they cannot access information to do.

Why it matters. This keeps Labour's campaign anchored on cost-of-living retail politics rather than the fiscal-credibility fight National wants to have. It also sets up a direct contrast with NZ First's fuel-subsidy pitch for the same driving-cost-sensitive voters.

1News · interest.co.nz · RNZ

CAMPAIGN
National pledges $800m for Christchurch Northern Motorway extension — but tolls still possible

Announcing near Kaiapoi with Luxon, transport spokesperson Chris Bishop said National would fully fund the Belfast to Pegasus Motorway and Woodend Bypass — an 11km extension of the Christchurch Northern Motorway — in the next government's first Budget, with construction starting next year. Bishop said tolling is being considered through a separate statutory process, with a decision likely in the new year; NZTA consulted on two toll points ($1.25 per light vehicle each), and Waimakariri mayor Dan Gordon pushed for the road to be toll-free. Newsroom noted the project was once estimated at $270m — roughly a quarter of the new price tag.

Why it matters. National is stacking regional roading commitments (after Mill Road on Sunday) to contrast with the Greens' rail platform and anchor its infrastructure-and-growth pitch in Canterbury. The unresolved tolling question and cost escalation give opponents an immediate line of attack.

RNZ · ODT · Newsroom

ISSUES
Edmonds fires back at Willis over pay-equity 'hole': 'The only liar there is Nicola Willis'

The fight over Labour's fiscal plan escalated as finance spokesperson Barbara Edmonds directly accused Nicola Willis of lying about the alleged $8.5 billion pay-equity gap. Edmonds argued neither governments nor oppositions should put a figure on pay equity because it is a bargaining envelope, and said the cost would come from Labour's $10.5 billion of unallocated funding. Winston Peters separately said of Labour's plan that it 'doesn't add up'.

Why it matters. The costings dispute has moved from technical disagreement to a direct credibility contest between the two would-be finance ministers. How this exchange is adjudicated by commentators and voters will shape whether the pay-equity hole becomes National's defining attack line for the final month.

PMN · RNZ

CAMPAIGN
Near miss with train interrupts Greens' level-crossing removal pledge

The Greens announced they would fund the removal of level crossings along Auckland's Western Line — enabling more than 12 trains an hour on the line and allowing the City Rail Link to run at its full 48-trains-an-hour capacity — when a car drove across a crossing in front of an approaching train mid-announcement. Swarbrick called it 'one of those very near misses' and said it proved the case for removal; she contrasted the investment with National's roading announcements. The pledge caps a Greens transport platform that also included a $4b intercity passenger rail plan announced 3 October (overnight Auckland–Wellington services, Christchurch–Dunedin trains, Te Huia extended to Tauranga).

Why it matters. The near miss gave the Greens a rare made-for-TV moment that dramatised their own policy argument. The party is running transport as its signature cost-of-living and infrastructure wedge against National's road-heavy programme.

RNZ · 1News · RNZ (rail plan, 3 Oct)

POLLS
CATCH-UP (1 Oct): MATA-Horizon poll has Greens edging Labour for the Māori-roll party vote

A MATA-Horizon poll released 1 October found the Greens leading the party vote among voters on the Māori roll with 28%, ahead of Labour (27%), Te Pāti Māori (16%) and Opportunity (6%); among Māori on the general roll, Labour led on 38% with the Greens at 17%, National 11% and NZ First 10%. Commentators including Tau Henare described the shift as 'massive'. On 5 October, Green co-leader Marama Davidson discussed the result on Waatea's Ata Tū alongside the party's rail announcements.

Why it matters. A Green lead on the Māori roll is historically unusual and complicates Te Pāti Māori's one-person-list, candidate-vote-only strategy, which depends on its supporters giving their party vote elsewhere. It also sharpens competition inside the left bloc for the same voters Labour needs to win the seven Māori electorates.

How the seats are worked out
The average

Each pollster's house effect — its systematic lean per party against the consensus of all pollsters over the past 18 months — is estimated and subtracted first. Adjusted polls are then combined with an exponential recency weight (half-life 30 days) scaled by sample size. Newer, larger polls count for more; pollsters that skip a party are left out of that party's average. The table below always shows the published numbers.

The seats

New Zealand uses MMP: the 120 seats are shared out by the Sainte-Laguë formula among parties that clear 5% of the party vote or win an electorate. A party that wins more electorates than its party vote would entitle it to keeps the extras, and Parliament grows — the overhang. The majority line moves with it.

The simulations

The forecast draws 10,000 possible election-day results around the adjusted average. Each party's error grows with its size and with the time left until 7 November, calibrated by replaying this exact method against the 2014, 2017, 2020 and 2023 elections — at this range, history says a major party's polling can miss by five points or more. Electorate seats are sampled too: an assumption switched on holds in 85% of simulations, one switched off still happens in 12%. Every draw runs through the same Sainte-Laguë maths, and the outcomes are counted.

The grand-coalition wildcard

No National–Labour government has ever formed under MMP, so its probability can't be measured from New Zealand data. Instead, each simulated parliament is classified by how cornered the majors are — a bloc governs outright (0.2% chance of a grand coalition), a kingmaker holds the balance (1% plus 0.15% per seat the kingmaker controls), or no aligned majority exists at all (20%, the situation that produced Germany's 2005 and Ireland's 2020 grand coalitions) — weighted by how evenly matched the majors are, and zeroed where they'd lack a majority together. Treat it as a structured hunch, not a measurement.

Electorate assumptions

Sub-5% parties live or die on electorate seats, so the switches under the seat map matter. Te Pāti Māori's five Māori electorates (down from six after Kapa-Kingi's departure) are a two-way switch — TPM holds them, or Labour sweeps them. ACT's Epsom is taken as read and is not a switch. Give TOP an electorate seat and the balance can flip. The Full right overhang preset loads the split-ticket play into the sandbox: National's party vote moved to ACT and NZ First, half each, with the 43 electorates it won in 2023 kept as pure overhang.
