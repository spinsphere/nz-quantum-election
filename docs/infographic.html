How it works, in three steps (no physics required)

1

Score the policies

Each party rated 1 to 10 in eight areas (health, education, justice, housing, economy, jobs, transport, Te Tiriti) on independent evidence: Treasury, Reserve Bank, OECD, Ministry evaluations, the Waitangi Tribunal.

2

Measure the friction

Every pair of parties gets a distance score for how far apart their positions sit. Like staffing a company: skill matters, but people who fight over everything burn the team's energy.

3

Find the best team that can govern

Highest total score minus friction, subject to reaching 62 seats on the six latest polls. The quantum computer searched the 128 possible coalitions; a classical check confirmed its answer.

Low friction inside the winning four is why it beats bigger coalitions. The biggest internal gaps are justice, Te Tiriti and tax, where only Labour's capital gains tax is costed inside a fiscal plan.

Three things to know before you share this

5%

The whole result hinges on TOP clearing the threshold. It polls between 4.5% and 9.5%. In every September projection with TOP under 5%, National, ACT and NZ First win a majority instead.

0.5–1.5

The answer is robust to how much disagreement is penalised. The same four parties win across this range of the friction coefficient. Only at twice the baseline does a National–Labour grand coalition take over.

+1

A one-seat buffer is fragile. With Te Pāti Māori's recent splits and a candidate-vote-only strategy, confidence and supply from outside cabinet is more likely than a formal four-party deal. National and TOP, and Labour and NZ First, have ruled each other out.
